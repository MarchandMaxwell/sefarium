Plato

The thirty-six works in the Perseus collection, in John Burnet’s Greek, arranged in the nine Thrasyllan tetralogies. Each link opens the full text with Stephanus numbers.

Tetralogy I (Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Phaedo)

EuthyphroΕὐθύφρων Socrates asks what piety is, outside the court where he will soon be tried. 2a–16a. ApologyἈπολογία Σωκράτους Socrates’ defence before the Athenian jury, and what he says after the verdict. 17a–42a. CritoΚρίτων In prison, Socrates explains why he will not escape. 43a–54e. PhaedoΦαίδων On the last day of Socrates’ life, a conversation about the soul. 57a–118a.

Tetralogy II (Cratylus, Theaetetus, Sophist, Statesman)

CratylusΚρατύλος Whether names are natural or conventional, and what they can teach. 383a–440e. TheaetetusΘεαίτητος What knowledge is, tried against perception, true belief, and an account. 142a–210d. SophistΣοφιστής The Eleatic stranger hunts the sophist and has to face not-being. 216a–268d. StatesmanΠολιτικός The stranger defines the statesman by dividing the arts of rule. 257a–311c.

Tetralogy III (Parmenides, Philebus, Symposium, Phaedrus)

ParmenidesΠαρμενίδης A young Socrates meets Parmenides, and then the one is put through its paces. 126a–166c. PhilebusΦίληβος Pleasure and knowledge, and which of them should rule a human life. 11a–67b. SymposiumΣυμπόσιον Speeches in praise of love, ending in Diotima’s account of it. 172a–223d. PhaedrusΦαῖδρος Love, rhetoric, and the soul, in a conversation by the Ilissus. 227a–279c.

Tetralogy IV (Alcibiades I and II, Hipparchus, Lovers)

Alcibiades IἈλκιβιάδης αʹ What Alcibiades must know before he leads Athens, and what the self is. An annotated edition is also on Sefarium. 103a–135e. Alcibiades IIἈλκιβιάδης βʹ A prayer before a sacrifice, and whether one knows what to ask of the gods. Traditionally doubted. 138a–151c. HipparchusἽππαρχος A short dialogue on the love of gain. Traditionally doubted. 225a–232c. LoversἘρασταί In a schoolroom, what philosophy is. Traditionally doubted. 132a–139a.

Tetralogy V (Theages, Charmides, Laches, Lysis)

TheagesΘεάγης A father asks Socrates to teach his son, and the divine sign comes into it. Traditionally doubted. 121a–131a. CharmidesΧαρμίδης Temperance, on the day Socrates returns from Potidaea. 153a–176d. LachesΛάχης Two generals and two fathers ask how courage should be taught. 178a–201c. LysisΛύσις Friendship, among the boys at a wrestling school. 203a–223b.

Tetralogy VI (Euthydemus, Protagoras, Gorgias, Meno)

EuthydemusΕὐθύδημος Two brothers show off eristic argument, and Socrates answers with care. 271a–307c. ProtagorasΠρωταγόρας Can virtue be taught? Socrates and Protagoras argue it before a crowd. 309a–362a. GorgiasΓοργίας Rhetoric and power, and whether it is better to suffer wrong than to do it. 447a–527e. MenoΜένων What virtue is, and whether it can be taught, with the slave’s geometry. 70a–100c.

Tetralogy VII (Hippias Major and Minor, Ion, Menexenus)

Hippias MajorἹππίας μείζων Socrates presses Hippias on what the beautiful is. 281a–304e. Hippias MinorἹππίας ἐλάττων Whether the person who errs on purpose is better than the one who does not. 363a–376c. IonἼων A rhapsode, and whether he speaks from an art or from inspiration. 530a–542b. MenexenusΜενέξενος Socrates recites a funeral speech he says he learned from Aspasia. 234a–249e.

Tetralogy VIII (Cleitophon, Republic, Timaeus, Critias)

CleitophonΚλειτοφῶν Cleitophon says Socrates exhorts well but will not teach. Traditionally doubted. 406a–410e. RepublicΠολιτεία Justice in the soul and in the city, told in ten books. 327a–621d. TimaeusΤίμαιος A likely account of the cosmos, the world-soul, and the human body. 17a–92c. CritiasΚριτίας The unfinished story of ancient Athens and Atlantis. 106a–121c.

Tetralogy IX (Minos, Laws, Epinomis, Letters)

MinosΜίνως What a law is. Traditionally doubted. 313a–321d. LawsΝόμοι An Athenian, a Cretan, and a Spartan plan a city and its laws. 624a–969d. EpinomisἘπινομίς A sequel to the Laws, on wisdom and the study of the heavens. Traditionally doubted. 973a–992e. LettersἘπιστολαί Thirteen letters attributed to Plato. They are not all regarded as genuine. 309a–363e.
