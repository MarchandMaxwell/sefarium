About This Text

Dramatic date c. 414 BC. Ion of Ephesus has just come from a contest. Ephesus is still spoken of as under Athens, so before the revolt of 412.

Socrates meets the rhapsode Ion of Ephesus, who has just won the prize at the festival of Asclepius in Epidaurus and is confident about Homer. Socrates asks whether Ion is equally clever about Hesiod and the other poets. Ion says he nods off when anyone speaks of them, and comes awake only for Homer. Socrates argues that if speaking well about poetry were a craft, the man who had it could handle every poet, since the craft is one. He then takes the passages where Homer talks of chariot racing, medicine, fishing, and generalship, and asks who can judge those lines, the rhapsode or the specialist. Ion claims that a good rhapsode is also a good general, and that he could command. Socrates says a man who can really do that would not be content to recite. He offers another account: a poet is held by a divine power, like an iron ring on a magnet, and the rhapsode and the audience are further rings in the same chain. Ion prefers to be called divinely possessed rather than dishonest. Socrates grants him the finer title.

Love in this dialogue

A prize-winning reciter of Homer admits he is moved by poetry but can't explain it. Socrates says he is possessed, like a link in a magnetic chain from the Muse. Passion is a divine gift, but it isn't knowledge.
