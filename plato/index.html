Plato

The thirty-six works in the Perseus collection, in John Burnet’s Greek. The list follows the story of Socrates’ life. Πλάτων

Thrasyllus’ nine groups of four, the order transmitted with the corpus. The date and setting of each conversation are still shown on the row.

Tetralogy I (Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Phaedo)

EuthyphroΕὐθύφρων399 BC. The porch of the King Archon, before the hearing of the indictment.Outside the court, Socrates meets Euthyphro, who is prosecuting his own father, and asks him what piety is. They can't decide whether the gods love what is holy because it's holy, or whether it's holy because they love it. Love has to aim at what is good in itself.ApologyἈπολογία Σωκράτους399 BC. The trial before the Athenian jury.Socrates defends himself before 500 Athenian jurors. He says the oracle at Delphi sent him to question everyone, and he won't stop, even to save himself. He is condemned to death and tells them no harm can come to a good man. He loves the truth and his city more than his life.CritoΚρίτων399 BC. The prison, before dawn. The sacred ship from Delos has been sighted.At dawn in prison, his old friend Crito says the escape is arranged and begs him to go. Socrates imagines the Laws of Athens speaking to him like parents, and he stays. Loyalty and love mean keeping faith even when it costs everything.PhaedoΦαίδων399 BC. The prison, on the day of Socrates’ death. Phaedo tells the story to Echecrates.On his last day, his friends gather. Socrates argues that the soul is immortal and that philosophy is a lifelong preparation for death. At sunset he drinks the hemlock calmly while his friends weep. The lover of wisdom goes home to what he loved all along.

Tetralogy II (Cratylus, Theaetetus, Sophist, Statesman)

CratylusΚρατύλοςDate uncertain. Hermogenes and Cratylus argue over names. Some take a reference to the Euthyphro as placing this talk on that same morning in 399.Two friends argue whether names are natural or just convention, and Socrates offers playful etymologies, including for "eros". Words point to the truth, but love has to reach the thing itself.TheaetetusΘεαίτητοςSpring 399 BC. Theodorus introduces Theaetetus. At the end Socrates must answer the indictment. Euclid reads the conversation aloud years later.Shortly before his trial, Socrates meets a gifted young mathematician and calls himself a midwife of souls. They test three definitions of knowledge and none survives. He loves the young by helping them birth their own thoughts and checking whether they're true.SophistΣοφιστής399 BC. The day after the Theaetetus. Theodorus brings an Eleatic stranger, as he had promised.A visitor from Elea, a pupil of Parmenides, tracks down the sophist, the imitator of wisdom, and in doing so works out how falsehood is possible. To love wisdom you have to tell it apart from its counterfeit.StatesmanΠολιτικός399 BC. The same day as the Sophist, continued. The younger Socrates takes Theaetetus’ place.The same visitor defines the true ruler as a weaver who binds bold and gentle citizens together. Love and friendship are the threads that make a city whole.

Tetralogy III (Parmenides, Philebus, Symposium, Phaedrus)

ParmenidesΠαρμενίδηςc. 450 BC. The Great Panathenaea. Socrates is about twenty.A very young Socrates presents his theory of Forms to the old philosopher Parmenides, who takes it apart and then runs him through a hard logical workout. Loving the truth means letting even your dearest ideas be tested.PhilebusΦίληβοςNo dramatic date. Socrates talks with Protarchus and Philebus, and nothing in the scene fixes a year. A proposal that this is spoken in the prison is not the usual view.Socrates and young Protarchus weigh pleasure against wisdom and decide the good life is a measured mixture of both. Desire is good when reason sets its measure.SymposiumΣυμπόσιον416 BC. Agathon’s house, the night after his first victory. Apollodorus retells the party some years later.At a drinking party for the poet Agathon, the guests each praise Love. Aristophanes says lovers are split halves seeking wholeness. Socrates tells what the priestess Diotima taught him: love is longing to possess the beautiful forever, rising from one body to all beauty, then to souls, laws and knowledge, and finally to Beauty itself. Then a drunk Alcibiades bursts in and confesses that Socrates refused his seduction. This is the centre of Plato on love.PhaedrusΦαῖδροςc. 418 BC. By the Ilissus, while Phaedrus is still in Athens. Nails argues for 418 or 416. Others put the walk in 404, after he returned from exile.Socrates and the young Phaedrus walk barefoot along a stream outside the city and lie down under a plane tree. Phaedrus reads a speech saying it's better to give yourself to someone who doesn't love you. Socrates answers that love is a divine madness: the soul is a charioteer with a noble horse and an unruly one, and the sight of the beloved regrows the soul's wings. Two lovers who rein in desire together rise toward the divine.

Tetralogy IV (Alcibiades I and II, Hipparchus, Lovers)

Alcibiades IἈλκιβιάδης αʹc. 432 BC. Alcibiades is about twenty, about to enter public life, before the campaign at Potidaea.Socrates finally speaks to the proud young Alcibiades, the lover who stayed after all the others left. He shows him he isn't ready to lead Athens because he doesn't know himself. To love someone is to love their soul and help it see itself.Alcibiades IIἈλκιβιάδης βʹc. 407 BC. Alcibiades is on his way to pray, after his return to Athens and before Notium. The dialogue quotes a play produced in 409. Traditionally doubted.Socrates meets Alcibiades on his way to pray and warns him that people often ask the gods for things that ruin them. Love without wisdom can wish harm on what it loves.HipparchusἽππαρχοςNo dramatic date. Nothing in the text fixes a year. A connection with the herms of 415 is only a guess. Traditionally doubted.Socrates presses a friend on whether love of gain is shameful and concludes that everyone loves the good, though few know what it is.LoversἘρασταίNo dramatic date. A schoolroom, with no historical marker. Traditionally doubted.In a school, two rivals compete for a boy's attention, one an athlete and one well read. Socrates asks whether loving wisdom means knowing a lot. It means knowing how to make oneself and others better.

Tetralogy V (Theages, Charmides, Laches, Lysis)

TheagesΘεάγηςc. 409 BC. Demodocus brings his son after the general Thrasyllus has left for Ionia. Traditionally doubted.A farmer brings his son to Socrates to learn wisdom. Socrates explains that his inner divine sign decides whom he can help. Being near a good teacher helps only when the student truly loves the good.CharmidesΧαρμίδης429 BC. Taureas’ palaestra, the morning after Socrates returns from Potidaea.Back from battle, Socrates meets the stunningly beautiful Charmides and catches a glimpse inside his cloak, which almost unsettles him. He steadies himself, and they try and fail to define self-control. Self-mastery is what lets love see instead of grab.LachesΛάχης424 BC. Soon after the retreat from Delium. Nicias and Laches are both still alive.Two fathers ask two generals, Laches and Nicias, whether their sons should learn armoured fighting. Socrates draws them into asking what courage is. To love something enough to stand firm for it, you must know what is truly worth fearing.LysisΛύσιςc. 421 BC. A new palaestra, at festival time. Menexenus is still a boy. The year is less firmly fixed than that of the Laches.At a wrestling school, Socrates helps the shy Hippothales, who is in love with the boy Lysis. Instead of flattering Lysis, Socrates talks with him and his friend about what makes someone a friend, and they can't settle it. Every love points past the beloved toward a "first beloved", the good itself.

Tetralogy VI (Euthydemus, Protagoras, Gorgias, Meno)

EuthydemusΕὐθύδημοςc. 412 BC. The Lyceum. Crito asks about two brothers he has just seen. Some prefer 418, before the exiles from Thurii arrived.Socrates recounts two brothers who win every argument with ridiculous word traps while a young man watches. Cleverness that cares about winning loves no one; real teaching cares whether the other person becomes wise.ProtagorasΠρωταγόραςc. 433 BC. Callias’ house. Alcibiades has just grown a beard. Nails allows 432 as well.Socrates is fresh from flirting with Alcibiades when a friend drags him off to hear the famous sophist Protagoras. They argue over whether virtue can be taught, and by the end each has swapped positions. Socrates tells the friend he forgot Alcibiades entirely, because wisdom is the greater beauty.GorgiasΓοργίαςc. 427 BC. Gorgias is in Athens on his embassy. The dialogue also mentions later events, and some readers place the talk in 405, after Socrates had sat on the Council.At a dinner, Socrates faces the orator Gorgias, then the hot-headed Polus, then the ruthless Callicles, who mocks him for preferring philosophy to power. Socrates answers that flattery feeds what people want, real care gives them what's good, and it is better to suffer wrong than to do it.MenoΜένων402 BC. Meno is Anytus’ guest in Athens, before he leaves with Cyrus’ expedition.The young Meno asks whether virtue can be taught. Socrates leads a slave boy to work out a geometry proof and argues that learning is recollection. Then Anytus, who will help prosecute him, warns him darkly. The love of learning is the soul remembering what it already longs for.

Tetralogy VII (Hippias Major and Minor, Ion, Menexenus)

Hippias MajorἹππίας μείζωνc. 420 BC. Hippias of Elis is in Athens, which the Peace of Nicias makes plausible.The vain sophist Hippias tries to define "the beautiful" and keeps failing under Socrates' questions. If you can't say what beauty is, love doesn't know what it's chasing.Hippias MinorἹππίας ἐλάττωνc. 420 BC. The same visit, after Hippias has given a display on Homer.Socrates argues with Hippias over whether honest Achilles or wily Odysseus is the better man, and reaches a troubling paradox about doing wrong on purpose. The heart has to be ordered toward the good, not just skilled.IonἼωνc. 414 BC. Ion of Ephesus has just come from a contest. Ephesus is still spoken of as under Athens, so before the revolt of 412.A prize-winning reciter of Homer admits he is moved by poetry but can't explain it. Socrates says he is possessed, like a link in a magnetic chain from the Muse. Passion is a divine gift, but it isn't knowledge.MenexenusΜενέξενοςc. 401 BC. Menexenus is thinking of a public career. The speech he hears narrates events down to the King’s Peace of 386, after Socrates’ death, so the date cannot be made fully consistent.Socrates recites a funeral speech he says he learned from Aspasia, praising the war dead. Love of country can be noble or mere flattery.

Tetralogy VIII (Cleitophon, Republic, Timaeus, Critias)

CleitophonΚλειτοφῶνNo dramatic date. A speech addressed to Socrates, with no settled year. Traditionally doubted.Cleitophon tells Socrates that he inspires people to virtue but never shows them how to reach it. Love of the good needs a path, not just a spark.RepublicΠολιτείαc. 411 BC. The Piraeus, at the new festival of Bendis. A date of 421, in the Peace of Nicias, and one of 429 are also defended. Nails argues that no single year fits every detail.At a festival night in the port of Piraeus, Socrates is pressed by the angry Thrasymachus and then by Plato's brothers to prove justice is worth having. He builds a perfect city in words to see justice large. Desire, educated by reason, learns to love the Good, which lights up everything worth loving like the sun. The tyrant is love gone wrong: endless appetite and no friends.TimaeusΤίμαιοςc. 411 BC. The next day, if the opening is read as continuing the Republic. The summary Socrates gives does not match that dialogue in every particular.The day after the Republic conversation, the astronomer Timaeus describes how a good craftsman made the cosmos to be as much like himself as possible. Creation itself is an act of generous love.CritiasΚριτίαςc. 411 BC. The same day, after Timaeus has finished.Critias begins the tale of ancient Athens and Atlantis, a proud empire that grew greedy and was punished by the gods. It breaks off unfinished. A people whose love turns to greed falls.

Tetralogy IX (Minos, Laws, Epinomis, Letters)

MinosΜίνωςNo dramatic date. Socrates and a companion, and no occasion is given. Traditionally doubted.Socrates asks a companion what law is and argues that true law aims at the truth. Lawful order is a form of care.LawsΝόμοιSocrates is not a character. An Athenian stranger walks in Crete with Clinias of Cnossus and the Spartan Megillus.Three old men, from Athens, Crete and Sparta, walk to a shrine of Zeus and draft laws for a new city. The lawgiver loves the citizens like a parent and leads mainly by persuasion and education, so that marriage, festivals and friendship bind the city together.EpinomisἘπινομίςSocrates is not a character. The same three speakers as in the Laws, continuing that conversation. Usually thought not to be by Plato.The same three men discuss the wisdom that crowns the laws, namely number and the study of the heavens. Wonder at the ordered sky is love turned toward the divine.LettersἘπιστολαίNot a scene from Socrates’ life. Thirteen letters attributed to Plato, mostly to Dionysius II and to Dion, after 399. They are not all regarded as genuine.Plato, now old, writes about his failed attempts to guide the rulers of Syracuse and his grief over his murdered friend Dion. Truth is kindled in the soul like a spark from long companionship, not from writing.

The dates are dramatic dates: the year of the conversation in the story, not the year Plato wrote the dialogue. The frame follows Debra Nails, The People of Plato (Indianapolis: Hackett, 2002), and the case for reading in dramatic order made by Catherine H. Zuckert, Plato’s Philosophers (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2010). The run from the Theaetetus to the Phaedo is fixed by what those dialogues say about the next day and the trial. The conventional setting of the Gorgias in 427 is that of E. R. Dodds, Plato: Gorgias (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959). For the Republic, see Debra Nails, “The Dramatic Date of Plato’s Republic,” Classical Journal 93 (1998). Where a year is still disputed, the more usual placement is printed and the alternative is named on the row. Narrower dates in the war years also use the internal evidence as set out by Christopher Planeaux.

About the order

This is not the order of writing. It is the order of the conversations in Socrates’ life, as far as the texts and the usual reconstructions allow. Years still in dispute are named on the row.
