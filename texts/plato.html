The thirty-six works in the Perseus collection, in John Burnet’s Greek. The list follows the story of Socrates’ life. Πλάτων

Thrasyllus’ nine groups of four, the order transmitted with the corpus. The date and setting of each conversation are still shown on the row.

Tetralogy I (Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Phaedo)

EuthyphroΕὐθύφρων399 BC. The porch of the King Archon, before the hearing of the indictment.Socrates is at the porch of the King Archon, about to answer the charge Meletus has brought, that he corrupts the young and invents new gods. He meets Euthyphro, who is there to prosecute his own father. A dependent of the family had killed a household slave in a drunken fight on Naxos. Euthyphro's father bound the man and threw him in a ditch while he sent to Athens to ask the religious authority what to do, and the prisoner died of neglect. The relatives say a son should not prosecute a father. Euthyphro is sure that he knows piety better than they do. Socrates asks to become his pupil, so that he can use the knowledge at his own trial. Euthyphro says piety is doing what he is doing, prosecuting a wrongdoer whoever it is. Socrates wants the form, the one thing that makes every pious act pious. Euthyphro says it is what the gods love. Socrates asks what happens when the gods quarrel. Euthyphro narrows it to what all the gods love. Socrates asks whether the gods love the pious because it is pious, or whether it is pious because they love it, and Euthyphro cannot keep the two apart. They try piety as a care of the gods, and as a part of justice, the part concerned with service to the gods, and they cannot say what that service produces. Euthyphro remembers he is in a hurry and leaves. Socrates protests that he has not learned what he hoped.Love: Outside the court, Socrates meets Euthyphro, who is prosecuting his own father, and asks him what piety is. They can't decide whether the gods love what is holy because it's holy, or whether it's holy because they love it. Love has to aim at what is good in itself.ApologyἈπολογία Σωκράτους399 BC. The trial before the Athenian jury.Socrates speaks to the jury of five hundred and one Athenians. He answers first the old accusers, the ones who have been saying for years that he studies things in the sky and under the earth and makes the weaker argument the stronger. He recalls the comic Socrates of Aristophanes. The source of the reputation, he says, was Chaerephon's question to the oracle at Delphi, which answered that no one was wiser than Socrates. He tested the oracle by questioning politicians, poets, and craftsmen, and found that each group thought it knew what it did not. He concluded that his wisdom was knowing that he did not know. He then answers Meletus, who has charged him, with Anytus and Lycon, of corrupting the young and of not recognising the gods of the city. He shows that Meletus has never cared about the education of the young, and that the charge about gods contradicts itself if Socrates believes in divine signs at all. He refuses to stop philosophising in exchange for acquittal. He mentions Potidaea, Amphipolis, and Delium, his stand against the illegal trial of the generals, and his refusal under the Thirty to arrest Leon of Salamis. He will not parade his sons and weep. The vote goes against him. Offered the chance to name a penalty, he says he deserves free meals in the Prytaneum, then, with his friends pledging the money, offers thirty minas. The jury sentences him to death. He tells those who condemned him that killing him will not stop the questions, and those who acquitted him that death is either a dreamless sleep or a move to a place where he can talk with the great dead. It is time to go, he to die and they to live.Love: Socrates defends himself before 500 Athenian jurors. He says the oracle at Delphi sent him to question everyone, and he won't stop, even to save himself. He is condemned to death and tells them no harm can come to a good man. He loves the truth and his city more than his life.CritoΚρίτων399 BC. The prison, before dawn. The sacred ship from Delos has been sighted.It is just before dawn in the prison. Crito has come early, bribed the guard, and is sitting by Socrates, surprised to find him still asleep. The sacred ship has been sighted off Sunium on its return from Delos. When it arrives, the executions delayed for the festival will go ahead, so Socrates is likely to die the day after tomorrow. Crito has arranged the escape. The informers will not be expensive, and friends abroad, including Simmias in Thebes and Cebes, have money ready. He says Socrates will leave his sons uneducated, and that Crito himself will look cowardly for having failed to save him. Socrates says he will not leave unless Crito can show that leaving is just. He will not follow the opinion of the many, only the opinion of the one who understands, as a man in training follows his doctor. He imagines the laws of Athens standing before him. They say they begat him, reared him, and gave him a share of every good thing. He was free to leave the city if he disliked its arrangements, and he has stayed seventy years. To run away now, against a verdict, would be to try to destroy the laws. Nor should he drag his friends into danger. Crito has nothing left to say. Socrates tells him to give up, and to follow the way the god is leading.Love: At dawn in prison, his old friend Crito says the escape is arranged and begs him to go. Socrates imagines the Laws of Athens speaking to him like parents, and he stays. Loyalty and love mean keeping faith even when it costs everything.PhaedoΦαίδων399 BC. The prison, on the day of Socrates’ death. Phaedo tells the story to Echecrates.Echecrates of Phlius asks Phaedo, who was there, to tell him how Socrates died. Phaedo describes the morning in the prison. The friends were let in early. Xanthippe was sitting with their little boy. Socrates asked Crito to have her taken home, and she left crying. Socrates, rubbing the leg from which the chain had just been undone, remarked on how pleasure and pain follow each other. He said a dream had ordered him to make music, and that he had been writing verses in case philosophy was not the music meant. Cebes asked, on behalf of the poet Evenus, why Socrates was willing to die. Socrates answered that a philosopher spends his life preparing for the separation of soul from body. Simmias and Cebes demand proofs. Socrates argues that the living come from the dead, that learning is recollection, and that the soul is more like the unseen than like the body. Simmias objects that the soul may be only the body's attunement. Cebes objects that it may outlast several bodies and still die at last. Socrates tells of abandoning the natural philosophers for an explanation by forms, the beautiful by beauty itself, and he answers both objections. A myth follows, of a great earth, of hollows where we live, and of the rivers that carry souls. He bathes so the women will not have to wash the body, sees his three children, and sends them away. The officer of the Eleven comes in weeping. The poison is brought. Socrates asks what one is supposed to do, drinks it, and walks until his legs grow heavy. He lies down, covers his face, and uncovers it once to tell Crito they owe a cock to Asclepius. Crito closes his mouth and his eyes.Love: On his last day, his friends gather. Socrates argues that the soul is immortal and that philosophy is a lifelong preparation for death. At sunset he drinks the hemlock calmly while his friends weep. The lover of wisdom goes home to what he loved all along.

Tetralogy II (Cratylus, Theaetetus, Sophist, Statesman)

CratylusΚρατύλοςDate uncertain. Hermogenes and Cratylus argue over names. Some take a reference to the Euthyphro as placing this talk on that same morning in 399.Hermogenes and Cratylus have been arguing, and Hermogenes asks Socrates to join them. Cratylus has been saying that Hermogenes is not really his name. Hermogenes holds that names are conventional: whatever a person or a city agrees to call a thing is its name. Cratylus holds that names belong to things by nature, and that a badly made name is not a name at all. Socrates tries the question as a toolmaker would. A name is an instrument for teaching and for separating being, and the man who makes names is a legislator who looks to the form of the name. He then offers a long run of etymologies, of gods, of elements, of virtues, and of love, many of them suggesting that the first name-givers believed everything is in flux. Hermogenes is delighted. Socrates then turns on that picture. If everything constantly flows, there is nothing stable to name and nothing stable to know. He also gets Cratylus to admit that some names are poorly made, and that if names can be wrong we cannot learn the truth about things simply by studying their names. Better to look at the things themselves. Cratylus still leans toward Heraclitus. They agree to think more and talk again.Love: Two friends argue whether names are natural or just convention, and Socrates offers playful etymologies, including for "eros". Words point to the truth, but love has to reach the thing itself.TheaetetusΘεαίτητοςSpring 399 BC. Theodorus introduces Theaetetus. At the end Socrates must answer the indictment. Euclid reads the conversation aloud years later.Euclid of Megara meets Terpsion and tells him that Theaetetus has been carried up from the camp at Corinth, badly wounded, and is dying. Euclid remembers a conversation Socrates had with Theaetetus when Theaetetus was a boy, which Socrates later recounted and Euclid wrote down. A slave reads the book. In it, Theodorus of Cyrene introduces Theaetetus, who looks like Socrates and is remarkable at mathematics. Socrates says he practises the art of his mother, Phaenarete the midwife, except that he delivers souls. He asks what knowledge is. Theaetetus first says it is geometry, cobbling, and the other crafts. Socrates wants the one thing they share. Theaetetus then tries perception. Socrates develops that answer through Protagoras, for whom each man is the measure of what is, and through Heraclitus, for whom everything flows, and then shows that if perception is knowledge we cannot even say what a person will experience next, or distinguish dreaming from waking in the way the theory needs. Theaetetus next tries true judgment, and then true judgment together with an account. Socrates compares the mind to a wax block and to an aviary, and he takes apart three meanings of giving an account. None of the definitions survives. Socrates says he must go to the King's Porch to meet the indictment Meletus has drawn up, and he asks Theodorus to meet him again in the morning.Love: Shortly before his trial, Socrates meets a gifted young mathematician and calls himself a midwife of souls. They test three definitions of knowledge and none survives. He loves the young by helping them birth their own thoughts and checking whether they're true.SophistΣοφιστής399 BC. The day after the Theaetetus. Theodorus brings an Eleatic stranger, as he had promised.Theodorus keeps the appointment made the day before and brings with him a stranger from Elea, a companion of the circle of Parmenides and Zeno. Socrates asks whether the visitor is a god in disguise, come to examine them, and then requests that he tell them what the sophist, the statesman, and the philosopher are, and whether the three names belong to one kind or to three. The stranger chooses Theaetetus as his partner and begins with the sophist. They divide arts, productive and acquisitive, hunting and exchange, and they corner a figure who deals in speeches, claims to dispute about everything, and produces appearances. The definition sticks on a problem Parmenides had forbidden them to face: a copy and a false statement seem to say what is not. The stranger argues that this prohibition cannot stand. He sets out the greatest kinds, being, rest, motion, sameness, and difference, and shows that difference lets us say that a thing is not, meaning that it is other than something else. A statement weaves a verb together with a name, and it is false when it says of what is something other than what is about it. With false speech possible, they can finish the hunt. The sophist is an imitator who works in private, by short question and answer, knowing that he does not know. The stranger says they must not relax until the statesman has been caught as well.Love: A visitor from Elea, a pupil of Parmenides, tracks down the sophist, the imitator of wisdom, and in doing so works out how falsehood is possible. To love wisdom you have to tell it apart from its counterfeit.StatesmanΠολιτικός399 BC. The same day as the Sophist, continued. The younger Socrates takes Theaetetus’ place.The Eleatic stranger continues at once, and young Socrates takes Theaetetus' place as the one who answers. They look for the statesman by dividing the art of herding living things, cutting down through land animals and two-footed animals until the human herd is left. The division has put the statesman in with merchants, farmers, doctors, and every other claimant who helps feed the herd. To correct the picture the stranger tells a myth. The cosmos turns now one way and now the other. In the age of Cronus a god tended human beings and the earth produced food of itself. In the present age the god has let go, the world runs backward, and people must care for themselves. The myth shows that the king they are defining is a human ruler of a human herd, not a divine shepherd. The stranger then takes weaving as a model. Weaving is easy to separate from the neighbouring arts of carding, spinning, and fulling, and it shows what a definition should look like. He distinguishes causes from contributory causes, and he introduces two kinds of measurement, one that compares greater and smaller with each other and one that measures them against due measure. Constitutions are then ranked. The true constitution is the one in which the person who knows rules, whether by law or without it, and whether the ruled consent or not. Monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy are imitations, better when they keep to law and worse when they do not. The statesman's own work is a kind of weaving. He binds the courageous natures and the moderate natures together, by education and by marriages, so that the city does not tear itself between rashness and caution.Love: The same visitor defines the true ruler as a weaver who binds bold and gentle citizens together. Love and friendship are the threads that make a city whole.

Tetralogy III (Parmenides, Philebus, Symposium, Phaedrus)

ParmenidesΠαρμενίδηςc. 450 BC. The Great Panathenaea. Socrates is about twenty.Cephalus of Clazomenae meets Adeimantus and Glaucon in the Athenian market and asks after their brother Antiphon, who once learned a conversation by heart. At Antiphon's house the old story is told. During the Great Panathenaea, Parmenides and Zeno were staying with Pythodorus. Zeno read aloud his book of arguments against the claim that things are many. Socrates, still very young, answered that things are many because they share in forms, while each form is one. Parmenides then took that answer apart. If a form is separate from the things that share in it, how do they share, and how could anyone know the form? He said Socrates still needed training, and he set him an exercise. Aristotle, the young man who would later be one of the Thirty, answered while Parmenides worked through the consequences of saying that the one is, and of saying that the one is not, both for the one and for the others. Hypothesis after hypothesis ended in opposite conclusions. Aristotle agreed at last that, take it as you will, the one and the others both are and are not, and both seem and do not seem, in every way. The dialogue stops on that result.Love: A very young Socrates presents his theory of Forms to the old philosopher Parmenides, who takes it apart and then runs him through a hard logical workout. Loving the truth means letting even your dearest ideas be tested.PhilebusΦίληβοςNo dramatic date. Socrates talks with Protarchus and Philebus, and nothing in the scene fixes a year. A proposal that this is spoken in the prison is not the usual view.Socrates is arguing with Philebus, who has maintained that pleasure is the good, and with Protarchus, who has taken over Philebus' side of the debate. They agree to look for the state of soul that can make a human life happy. Socrates says that if pleasure is the whole good, a life of the greatest pleasures with no thought would do, and if wisdom is the whole good, a life of pure thought with no feeling would do. Protarchus admits he would not choose a pleasure that he could not perceive, and Socrates admits that no one would choose a wisdom that never felt joy. So the good life is a mixture. They then ask what kind of mixture, and which ingredient is more responsible for its goodness. Socrates sets out four kinds: the unlimited, the limit, the mixture of the two, and the cause of the mixture. Pleasure belongs mostly with the unlimited. Reason is on the side of the cause. He argues that some pleasures are false, because they rest on a false belief about what is to come, and that the violent pleasures are mixed with pain. The pure pleasures, such as those of learning and of beautiful colours and sounds, are the ones fit to be admitted. At the end they rank the goods. Measure and proportion come first, then reason and intelligence, then the sciences, and the pure pleasures after them. Philebus' candidate, bodily pleasure, comes last among the things allowed into the good life.Love: Socrates and young Protarchus weigh pleasure against wisdom and decide the good life is a measured mixture of both. Desire is good when reason sets its measure.SymposiumΣυμπόσιον416 BC. Agathon’s house, the night after his first victory. Apollodorus retells the party some years later.Apollodorus is stopped on the road by friends who want the story of Agathon's party. He had told it recently to Glaucon, and he had it from Aristodemus, who was there. On the night after Agathon won his first prize, Aristodemus met Socrates washed and in sandals, a rare sight, and was invited along. Socrates fell behind, standing on a neighbour's porch, and Aristodemus arrived alone. When Socrates came in they dined. Pausanias proposed that they drink lightly, having been drunk the night before, and send the flute girl away, each man giving a speech in praise of love. Phaedrus speaks of love as the oldest god and the source of honour. Pausanias divides a vulgar love from a heavenly one. Eryximachus, after curing Aristophanes' hiccups, makes love a principle of harmony in bodies and in the cosmos. Aristophanes tells of round people split in half, each half searching for its other. Agathon gives a polished praise of the god. Socrates says he cannot praise in that style. He reports the priestess Diotima: love is not beautiful or wise but a daimon between gods and mortals, child of Poverty and Resource, and it is the desire to possess the good forever. A lover can climb from one beautiful body to all beautiful bodies, then to souls, laws, and knowledge, and at last to beauty itself. Alcibiades then crashes in, drunk and crowned, and instead of praising love he praises Socrates, telling of the night Socrates refused his seduction, and of Potidaea and the retreat from Delium. A crowd of revellers bursts in. Near dawn Socrates is still arguing that the same man should be able to write tragedy and comedy. The others fall asleep. He gets up, spends the day as usual, and goes home in the evening.Love: At a drinking party for the poet Agathon, the guests each praise Love. Aristophanes says lovers are split halves seeking wholeness. Socrates tells what the priestess Diotima taught him: love is longing to possess the beautiful forever, rising from one body to all beauty, then to souls, laws and knowledge, and finally to Beauty itself. Then a drunk Alcibiades bursts in and confesses that Socrates refused his seduction. This is the centre of Plato on love.PhaedrusΦαῖδροςc. 418 BC. By the Ilissus, while Phaedrus is still in Athens. Nails argues for 418 or 416. Others put the walk in 404, after he returned from exile.Phaedrus has spent the morning with Lysias and is walking outside the walls to learn by heart a speech Lysias wrote. Socrates meets him, sees he is hiding the scroll, and makes him read it under a plane tree by the Ilissus. The speech argues that a boy should grant his favours to a man who is not in love with him, because a lover is jealous, unsteady, and damaging. Socrates, claiming he can do better, covers his head and gives a speech of his own to the same effect. As he is about to cross the stream, his divine sign stops him. He recants. Love, he now says, is a madness sent by the gods. The soul is like a charioteer with two horses, one noble and one unruly. Souls that follow a god around the rim of heaven glimpse the forms, and when they fall and are reborn the sight of a beautiful person makes their wings begin to grow again. Cicadas sing over the two men, and Socrates says the insects are watching to see whether they talk through the noon or fall asleep. They then judge Lysias' speech as rhetoric. A good speech needs an order, like a living body, and the speaker needs to know souls the way a doctor knows bodies. Writing, they agree, is only a reminder, weaker than talk that can answer. Socrates prays to Pan as they leave.Love: Socrates and the young Phaedrus walk barefoot along a stream outside the city and lie down under a plane tree. Phaedrus reads a speech saying it's better to give yourself to someone who doesn't love you. Socrates answers that love is a divine madness: the soul is a charioteer with a noble horse and an unruly one, and the sight of the beloved regrows the soul's wings. Two lovers who rein in desire together rise toward the divine.

Tetralogy IV (Alcibiades I and II, Hipparchus, Lovers)

Alcibiades IἈλκιβιάδης αʹc. 432 BC. Alcibiades is about twenty, about to enter public life, before the campaign at Potidaea.Socrates meets Alcibiades and says he has not spoken to him all these years because a divine sign held him back, and the sign has only now let him approach. Alcibiades is about to address the Assembly and is sure he can advise Athens on war and peace. Socrates asks what he would be advising about, and what he knows that the Assembly does not. Alcibiades says he knows what is just, then admits he never learned it and never found he lacked it. Socrates leads him to say that the just and the advantageous are the same, and that he has been answering from his own confusion. The talk turns to what it is to care for oneself rather than for one's belongings. The user of a thing is different from the thing he uses, so the man is the soul that uses the body. To know oneself is to look into another soul, as an eye sees itself in the pupil of an eye. Alcibiades agrees that he must be educated before he rules, and that if the city corrupts him it will be the stronger of the two.Love: Socrates finally speaks to the proud young Alcibiades, the lover who stayed after all the others left. He shows him he isn't ready to lead Athens because he doesn't know himself. To love someone is to love their soul and help it see itself.Alcibiades IIἈλκιβιάδης βʹc. 407 BC. Alcibiades is on his way to pray, after his return to Athens and before Notium. The dialogue quotes a play produced in 409. Traditionally doubted.Socrates meets Alcibiades as he is walking to pray, and asks whether he has considered the danger of the prayer he is about to make. People often beg the gods for marriage, office, or the death of an enemy, and then curse the day they received it. He reminds him of Oedipus, whose prayer that his sons divide the inheritance by the sword was fulfilled in the worst way. They talk about a kind of ignorance that is worse than ordinary ignorance: not knowing that one does not know what is good. Socrates praises the Spartans, who ask the gods only for what is noble and good, and leave the choice of the gift to them. Alcibiades is persuaded to put the sacrifice off. He takes the garland he had prepared and places it on Socrates instead.Love: Socrates meets Alcibiades on his way to pray and warns him that people often ask the gods for things that ruin them. Love without wisdom can wish harm on what it loves.HipparchusἽππαρχοςNo dramatic date. Nothing in the text fixes a year. A connection with the herms of 415 is only a guess. Traditionally doubted.Socrates is talking with a friend who has just spoken scornfully of lovers of gain. Socrates asks what the lover of gain loves. Gain, they agree, is the opposite of loss, and a loss is being deprived of something good. So gain is something good, and the lover of gain loves the good. But then everyone is a lover of gain, since everyone wants what benefits him, and the reproach makes no sense. The friend resists. Socrates tells him about Hipparchus, son of Peisistratus, who ruled Athens, educated the citizens, brought the poet Anacreon to the city, and set up herms on the roads inscribed with wise sayings of his own, so that people would admire his wisdom rather than the Delphic maxims. Hipparchus was later killed by Harmodius and Aristogeiton. The story is meant to show a man who loved gain of a respectable kind, the gain of being thought wise. The friend is not persuaded. They circle back over the same argument, and Socrates concludes that they should not slander the lover of gain. The friend agrees only that they should say what they think, and the piece ends there.Love: Socrates presses a friend on whether love of gain is shameful and concludes that everyone loves the good, though few know what it is.LoversἘρασταίNo dramatic date. A schoolroom, with no historical marker. Traditionally doubted.Socrates goes into the school of Dionysius the grammarian and finds two boys arguing, drawing circles and imitating the angles with their hands, apparently over Anaxagoras or Oenopides. He asks the young man beside him, a lover of one of the boys, what they are so serious about. The lover says it is idle chatter about things in the sky. Another young man, a rival for the same boy and a student of music, interrupts and says philosophy is a fine thing. Socrates asks them what philosophy is. The athletic lover treats it as a smattering of many arts, enough to follow a conversation and not enough to be a craftsman. Socrates compares that person to the pentathlete, who loses to the specialist in each event. He asks what knowledge would let someone assign each specialist his work and judge whether it is well done. They arrive at a knowledge of how to make human beings better, the knowledge a good ruler would have, and they agree that this, rather than dabbling, is what philosophy comes to. Socrates leaves the point there.Love: In a school, two rivals compete for a boy's attention, one an athlete and one well read. Socrates asks whether loving wisdom means knowing a lot. It means knowing how to make oneself and others better.

Tetralogy V (Theages, Charmides, Laches, Lysis)

TheagesΘεάγηςc. 409 BC. Demodocus brings his son after the general Thrasyllus has left for Ionia. Traditionally doubted.Demodocus of the deme Anagyrus comes into the city with his son Theages and finds Socrates. The boy wants to become wise, by which he means great among the Athenians, and he has been nagging his father to place him with a sophist. Socrates asks what he wants to learn, and shows him that the companions of Pericles and the other politicians have not picked up their skill by sitting beside them. Theages still wants Socrates himself. Socrates says he has no craft to teach. What he has is a divine sign, a voice that from childhood has turned him away from things he is about to do, and that also decides which of his companions are helped by being with him. Some improve the whole time they stay, and lose the benefit when they leave. Theages proposes that they try the association and see what the sign does. The dialogue ends on that proposal.Love: A farmer brings his son to Socrates to learn wisdom. Socrates explains that his inner divine sign decides whom he can help. Being near a good teacher helps only when the student truly loves the good.CharmidesΧαρμίδης429 BC. Taureas’ palaestra, the morning after Socrates returns from Potidaea.Socrates comes back from the camp at Potidaea and goes straight to Taureas' palaestra. Chaerephon greets him, and Critias tells him about the beautiful Charmides, who is said to be temperate as well. Charmides enters and the room turns to look. Socrates is asked to pretend he has a cure for the boy's headache. Looking inside Charmides' cloak, he is thrown off balance, then recovers and says the charm only works if the soul is treated first. He questions Charmides on what temperance is. The boy tries quietness, then modesty, then doing one's own work, and Critias takes over the last of these. Critias offers self-knowledge, and then a knowledge that knows itself and every other knowledge. Socrates shows that such a knowledge would not, by itself, give us the knowledge of good and bad that actually benefits us. They reach no definition. Critias tells Charmides to attach himself to Socrates, and Charmides says he will use force if he has to. Socrates says there is no resisting the two of them.Love: Back from battle, Socrates meets the stunningly beautiful Charmides and catches a glimpse inside his cloak, which almost unsettles him. He steadies himself, and they try and fail to define self-control. Self-mastery is what lets love see instead of grab.LachesΛάχης424 BC. Soon after the retreat from Delium. Nicias and Laches are both still alive.Lysimachus, son of Aristides, and Melesias, son of Thucydides, have watched a man display fighting in armour and ask Nicias and Laches whether their own sons should learn it. They are ashamed that their famous fathers left them no distinction. The two generals disagree, and Lysimachus turns to Socrates, who was with them at the retreat from Delium. Socrates says the question is not the instructor but the thing the boys are meant to acquire, and that thing is the care of their souls. They must therefore say what courage is. Laches tries standing one's ground in battle, then a kind of endurance. Socrates shows that some courageous acts are retreats, and that endurance without wisdom is not courage. Nicias offers knowledge of what is and is not to be feared, for a person and for a city. That account spreads until it seems to cover all of virtue, and Nicias cannot keep it to courage alone. They are all at a loss. Socrates says they should look for a teacher, and the others agree to meet him again.Love: Two fathers ask two generals, Laches and Nicias, whether their sons should learn armoured fighting. Socrates draws them into asking what courage is. To love something enough to stand firm for it, you must know what is truly worth fearing.LysisΛύσιςc. 421 BC. A new palaestra, at festival time. Menexenus is still a boy. The year is less firmly fixed than that of the Laches.Socrates is walking from the Academy to the Lyceum when Hippothales calls him over to a newly built palaestra. Hippothales is in love with the boy Lysis, writes poems about him and his ancestors, and blushes when Ctesippus tells on him. Socrates says that kind of praise only makes the beloved harder to catch. They go inside. Socrates talks with Lysis about why his parents, who love him, still will not let him do as he likes: they trust him where he has knowledge and restrain him where he does not. He then calls over Menexenus and asks what a friend is. They try out likeness, unlikeness, the presence of the good, and desire for what one lacks. Each account breaks. The servants come to take the boys home. Socrates gets in a last word: he and the two boys seem to be friends, and the others think they are, and yet they have not been able to discover what a friend is.Love: At a wrestling school, Socrates helps the shy Hippothales, who is in love with the boy Lysis. Instead of flattering Lysis, Socrates talks with him and his friend about what makes someone a friend, and they can't settle it. Every love points past the beloved toward a "first beloved", the good itself.

Tetralogy VI (Euthydemus, Protagoras, Gorgias, Meno)

EuthydemusΕὐθύδημοςc. 412 BC. The Lyceum. Crito asks about two brothers he has just seen. Some prefer 418, before the exiles from Thurii arrived.Crito asks Socrates who the two men were that he saw him talking with the day before in the Lyceum, inside a crowd. Hippias MajorἹππίας μείζωνc. 420 BC. Hippias of Elis is in Athens, which the Peace of Nicias makes plausible.Hippias of Elis arrives boastful. He has been serving his city as an envoy, making money by his speeches, and he looks down on the old wise men who never earned a fee. Socrates says he envies that confidence, because a man at home keeps asking him a blunt question and laughing at his answers. The question is what the beautiful is. Hippias first offers a beautiful girl, then gold, then to be rich, healthy, honoured, to bury one's parents, and to be buried by one's children. Socrates, speaking for the man at home, shows that each of these is an example or a case, not what beauty is in itself. They try the fitting, the useful, the favourable, and the pleasant that comes through eye and ear. Each proposal fails, sometimes because it leaves out beautiful things and sometimes because it cannot say what the pleasures have in common. Hippias grows angry at the quibbling. Socrates ends with the proverb that beautiful things are difficult.Love: The vain sophist Hippias tries to define "the beautiful" and keeps failing under Socrates' questions. Tetralogy VIII (Cleitophon, Republic, Timaeus, Critias)

CleitophonΚλειτοφῶνNo dramatic date. A speech addressed to Socrates, with no settled year. Traditionally doubted.Socrates asks Cleitophon whether he has heard rightly that Cleitophon, in talks with Lysias, criticises Socrates' conversations and praises the teaching of Thrasymachus. Cleitophon says he has been partly critical, and he asks leave to set the criticism out. Socrates agrees. Cleitophon then speaks at length and Socrates does not answer. He grants that Socrates is unmatched at waking people up, rebuking them for caring about money rather than about virtue, and turning them toward justice. What he cannot get, he says, is the next step. When he asked Socrates' companions what justice is and how one acquires it, they told him that justice is the beneficial, or friendship in cities, or the same thing named again. None of them could say what the product of justice is, the way a doctor can say that the product of medicine is health. He challenged Socrates himself and got nothing straight. He is ready to be Socrates' pupil if someone will teach him. Otherwise he will go to Thrasymachus and the others who claim they can. The dialogue ends on that speech.Love: Cleitophon tells Socrates that he inspires people to virtue but never shows them how to reach it. Love of the good needs a path, not just a spark.RepublicΠολιτείαc. 411 BC. The Piraeus, at the new festival of Bendis. A date of 421, in the Peace of Nicias, and one of 429 are also defended. Nails argues that no single year fits every detail.Socrates goes down to the Piraeus for the first festival of the Thracian goddess Bendis. On the way back, Polemarchus sends a slave to stop him, and Socrates spends the evening at the house of Cephalus, Polemarchus' father. The old man talks about wealth and old age and leaves to sacrifice. The conversation becomes an argument about justice. Polemarchus says it is helping friends and harming enemies. Socrates shows that a just man does not harm anyone. Thrasymachus breaks in, angry, and defines justice as the advantage of the stronger, the interest of whatever party rules. Socrates answers that rulers can mistake their interest, and that every craft aims at the good of its subject, not of the craftsman. Glaucon and Adeimantus are not satisfied. Glaucon tells of Gyges' ring, which makes a man invisible, and demands a proof that justice is worth having for itself, even if the just man is thought wicked and the unjust man is thought good. To see justice written in larger letters, Socrates builds a city in speech. He describes its guardians, their education in music and gymnastics, the noble lie about metals in the soul, and the life of the rulers, who hold property and families in common. The city will be saved only if philosophers rule or rulers become philosophers. He explains the philosopher by the images of the sun, the divided line, and the cave, then traces how cities and souls decline through oligarchy and democracy into tyranny. The tyrant, enslaved to appetite, is the most miserable of men. The last book argues that the soul is immortal and closes with the myth of Er: souls choose their next lives, and the choice is everything.Love: At a festival night in the port of Piraeus, Socrates is pressed by the angry Thrasymachus and then by Plato's brothers to prove justice is worth having. He builds a perfect city in words to see justice large. Desire, educated by reason, learns to love the Good, which lights up everything worth loving like the sun. The tyrant is love gone wrong: endless appetite and no friends.TimaeusΤίμαιοςc. 411 BC. The next day, if the opening is read as continuing the Republic. Critias says he can do it. He ends by saying how the souls of cowardly or light-minded men are reborn as women and then as animals, until the account of the universe is complete down to mortal creatures.Love: The day after the Republic conversation, the astronomer Timaeus describes how a good craftsman made the cosmos to be as much like himself as possible. A people whose love turns to greed falls.

Tetralogy IX (Minos, Laws, Epinomis, Letters)

MinosΜίνωςNo dramatic date. Socrates and a companion, and no occasion is given. Traditionally doubted.Socrates asks a companion what law is. The companion says it is the things that are accepted as lawful. Socrates points out that speech is not the things that are spoken, nor sight the things that are seen, and he asks again. The companion says law is the resolution of a city. Socrates answers that some resolutions are good and some are wicked, while law is meant to be something worthy, and that different cities and the same city at different times resolve opposite things, although what is real does not change in that way. He compares law to the finding of a doctor or a farmer who knows the subject. Law, he says, wants to be the discovery of what is. The companion is startled to hear Minos, the Cretan king, praised, because tragedy presents him as savage. Socrates says Minos conversed every nine years with Zeus, and that he gave Crete the laws which Lycurgus later studied for Sparta. Homer and Hesiod praise him. The Athenian tragedians slandered him because Athens had gone to war with Crete. The companion asks what makes a good lawgiver. Socrates says the one who can sow virtue in souls. The talk breaks off as he returns to who that person is.Love: Socrates asks a companion what law is and argues that true law aims at the truth. Lawful order is a form of care.LawsΝόμοιSocrates is not a character. An Athenian stranger walks in Crete with Clinias of Cnossus and the Spartan Megillus.Three old men walk the long road in Crete from Cnossus toward the cave and temple of Zeus. They are an Athenian stranger, Clinias of Crete, and Megillus of Sparta. The Athenian asks whether a god or a man is given the credit for their laws, and the talk opens into a day-long argument about what law is for. Clinias says the laws of Crete aim at victory in war, because every city is always at war with every other. The Athenian answers that the best law looks to the whole of virtue, not to courage alone, and that peace, not war, is the serious thing. He even defends supervised drinking as a way of testing character, and he uses the histories of Persia and of Athens to show how freedom and authority have to be held together. Clinias then admits he has been appointed, with nine others, to frame laws for a new colony, and he asks the Athenian to build the city in speech with him. They fix the households, the land, marriage, schooling, and the officials. Every law is to open with a prelude, so that a citizen obeys because he understands and not only because he is afraid. Later the Athenian argues, against those who say the world is only matter, that soul, which moves itself, is older than body, and that the gods care for human affairs. He sets out the punishments for impiety, theft, and murder. The work ends with a nocturnal council of the oldest and the best, trained in mathematics and in the single aim of the laws, and with the question of how that council is to be given the power to keep the city pointed at virtue.Love: Three old men, from Athens, Crete and Sparta, walk to a shrine of Zeus and draft laws for a new city. The lawgiver loves the citizens like a parent and leads mainly by persuasion and education, so that marriage, festivals and friendship bind the city together.EpinomisἘπινομίςSocrates is not a character. The same three speakers as in the Laws, continuing that conversation. Usually thought not to be by Plato.The Athenian stranger, Clinias, and Megillus meet again, as they had agreed, to say what knowledge a mortal needs if he is to be wise. Clinias recalls that they had promised to find the study which makes a human being good. The Athenian reviews the arts that people ordinarily admire. Farming, building, hunting, imitation, medicine, navigation, and generalship are all useful, and none of them is wisdom. He turns to number. A creature that could not count could not be brave, temperate, or just, and would have no account to give of anything. Number was given to human beings so that they could think. The highest use of it is the study of the heavens, the movements of the stars, which are the fairest and most orderly of visible things and are themselves divine. The Athenian describes the regions of the cosmos and the living beings proper to each, down to the daimons who mediate between gods and humans. Wisdom, he concludes, is piety instructed by astronomy. Without that study the guardian council they had planned will not have the knowledge it needs. The dialogue ends with Clinias agreeing that the stranger must be made to join the council.Love: The same three men discuss the wisdom that crowns the laws, namely number and the study of the heavens. Wonder at the ordered sky is love turned toward the divine.LettersἘπιστολαίNot a scene from Socrates’ life. Thirteen letters attributed to Plato, mostly to Dionysius II and to Dion, after 399. They are not all regarded as genuine.Thirteen letters are preserved under Plato's name, most of them addressed to Dionysius II, tyrant of Syracuse, or to Dion, Plato's friend at that court, and a few to other Greeks of the same years. They are not a single conversation, and they are not all of one quality. The Seventh is the long one. It answers the friends of Dion, who has been murdered, and it tells Plato's own story: his disillusion with the Thirty Tyrants in Athens, his judgment that the cities of his time were all badly governed, and his repeated visits to Syracuse, first under Dionysius I and then, at Dion's urging, under Dionysius II. He describes the philosophical test he set the younger Dionysius, the ruler's shallow enthusiasm, the quarrels, Plato's detention in the palace, and his eventual escape. In the middle of the narrative he pauses to say what he has never written. The things he really cares about cannot be put in a book. Understanding is kindled in the soul by long companionship, suddenly, like a light struck from a fire. He also warns Dion's party against taking revenge in a way that would repeat the disease they had meant to cure. The Eighth letter continues that political advice, urging a compromise in Syracuse among royal power, law, and freedom. The shorter letters give recommendations, rebukes, and requests. Several of them are generally thought to be later school exercises written in Plato's name.Love: Plato, now old, writes about his failed attempts to guide the rulers of Syracuse and his grief over his murdered friend Dion. Truth is kindled in the soul like a spark from long companionship, not from writing.

Youth Before the Peloponnesian War

ParmenidesΠαρμενίδηςc. 450 BC. The Great Panathenaea. Socrates is about twenty.Cephalus of Clazomenae meets Adeimantus and Glaucon in the Athenian market and asks after their brother Antiphon, who once learned a conversation by heart. At Antiphon's house the old story is told. During the Great Panathenaea, Parmenides and Zeno were staying with Pythodorus. Zeno read aloud his book of arguments against the claim that things are many. Socrates, still very young, answered that things are many because they share in forms, while each form is one. Parmenides then took that answer apart. If a form is separate from the things that share in it, how do they share, and how could anyone know the form? He said Socrates still needed training, and he set him an exercise. Aristotle, the young man who would later be one of the Thirty, answered while Parmenides worked through the consequences of saying that the one is, and of saying that the one is not, both for the one and for the others. Hypothesis after hypothesis ended in opposite conclusions. Aristotle agreed at last that, take it as you will, the one and the others both are and are not, and both seem and do not seem, in every way. The dialogue stops on that result.Love: A very young Socrates presents his theory of Forms to the old philosopher Parmenides, who takes it apart and then runs him through a hard logical workout. The Peloponnesian War From Potidaea to the defeat of Athens

Alcibiades IἈλκιβιάδης αʹc. 432 BC. Alcibiades is about twenty, about to enter public life, before the campaign at Potidaea.Socrates meets Alcibiades and says he has not spoken to him all these years because a divine sign held him back, and the sign has only now let him approach. Alcibiades is about to address the Assembly and is sure he can advise Athens on war and peace. Socrates asks what he would be advising about, and what he knows that the Assembly does not. Alcibiades says he knows what is just, then admits he never learned it and never found he lacked it. Socrates leads him to say that the just and the advantageous are the same, and that he has been answering from his own confusion. The talk turns to what it is to care for oneself rather than for one's belongings. The user of a thing is different from the thing he uses, so the man is the soul that uses the body. To know oneself is to look into another soul, as an eye sees itself in the pupil of an eye. Alcibiades agrees that he must be educated before he rules, and that if the city corrupts him it will be the stronger of the two.Love: Socrates finally speaks to the proud young Alcibiades, the lover who stayed after all the others left. Critias offers self-knowledge, and then a knowledge that knows itself and every other knowledge. Socrates shows that such a knowledge would not, by itself, give us the knowledge of good and bad that actually benefits us. They reach no definition. He will not follow the opinion of the many, only the opinion of the one who understands, as a man in training follows his doctor. He imagines the laws of Athens standing before him. They say they begat him, reared him, and gave him a share of every good thing. He was free to leave the city if he disliked its arrangements, and he has stayed seventy years. To run away now, against a verdict, would be to try to destroy the laws. Nor should he drag his friends into danger. Crito has nothing left to say. Socrates tells him to give up, and to follow the way the god is leading.Love: At dawn in prison, his old friend Crito says the escape is arranged and begs him to go. The prison, on the day of Socrates’ death. Phaedo tells the story to Echecrates.Echecrates of Phlius asks Phaedo, who was there, to tell him how Socrates died. Phaedo describes the morning in the prison. The friends were let in early. Xanthippe was sitting with their little boy. Socrates asked Crito to have her taken home, and she left crying. Socrates, rubbing the leg from which the chain had just been undone, remarked on how pleasure and pain follow each other. He said a dream had ordered him to make music, and that he had been writing verses in case philosophy was not the music meant. Cebes asked, on behalf of the poet Evenus, why Socrates was willing to die. Socrates answered that a philosopher spends his life preparing for the separation of soul from body. Simmias and Cebes demand proofs. Socrates argues that the living come from the dead, that learning is recollection, and that the soul is more like the unseen than like the body. Simmias objects that the soul may be only the body's attunement. Cebes objects that it may outlast several bodies and still die at last. Socrates tells of abandoning the natural philosophers for an explanation by forms, the beautiful by beauty itself, and he answers both objections. A myth follows, of a great earth, of hollows where we live, and of the rivers that carry souls. He bathes so the women will not have to wash the body, sees his three children, and sends them away. The officer of the Eleven comes in weeping. The poison is brought. Socrates asks what one is supposed to do, drinks it, and walks until his legs grow heavy. He lies down, covers his face, and uncovers it once to tell Crito they owe a cock to Asclepius. Crito closes his mouth and his eyes.Love: On his last day, his friends gather. Socrates argues that the soul is immortal and that philosophy is a lifelong preparation for death. At sunset he drinks the hemlock calmly while his friends weep. The lover of wisdom goes home to what he loved all along.

PhilebusΦίληβοςNo dramatic date. Socrates talks with Protarchus and Philebus, and nothing in the scene fixes a year. A proposal that this is spoken in the prison is not the usual view.Socrates is arguing with Philebus, who has maintained that pleasure is the good, and with Protarchus, who has taken over Philebus' side of the debate. They agree to look for the state of soul that can make a human life happy. Socrates says that if pleasure is the whole good, a life of the greatest pleasures with no thought would do, and if wisdom is the whole good, a life of pure thought with no feeling would do. Protarchus admits he would not choose a pleasure that he could not perceive, and Socrates admits that no one would choose a wisdom that never felt joy. So the good life is a mixture. They then ask what kind of mixture, and which ingredient is more responsible for its goodness. Socrates sets out four kinds: the unlimited, the limit, the mixture of the two, and the cause of the mixture. Pleasure belongs mostly with the unlimited. Reason is on the side of the cause. He argues that some pleasures are false, because they rest on a false belief about what is to come, and that the violent pleasures are mixed with pain. The pure pleasures, such as those of learning and of beautiful colours and sounds, are the ones fit to be admitted. At the end they rank the goods. Measure and proportion come first, then reason and intelligence, then the sciences, and the pure pleasures after them. Philebus' candidate, bodily pleasure, comes last among the things allowed into the good life.Love: Socrates and young Protarchus weigh pleasure against wisdom and decide the good life is a measured mixture of both. Desire is good when reason sets its measure.HipparchusἽππαρχοςNo dramatic date. Nothing in the text fixes a year. A connection with the herms of 415 is only a guess. Traditionally doubted.Socrates is talking with a friend who has just spoken scornfully of lovers of gain. Socrates asks what the lover of gain loves. Gain, they agree, is the opposite of loss, and a loss is being deprived of something good. So gain is something good, and the lover of gain loves the good. But then everyone is a lover of gain, since everyone wants what benefits him, and the reproach makes no sense. The friend resists. Socrates tells him about Hipparchus, son of Peisistratus, who ruled Athens, educated the citizens, brought the poet Anacreon to the city, and set up herms on the roads inscribed with wise sayings of his own, so that people would admire his wisdom rather than the Delphic maxims. Hipparchus was later killed by Harmodius and Aristogeiton. The story is meant to show a man who loved gain of a respectable kind, the gain of being thought wise. The friend is not persuaded. They circle back over the same argument, and Socrates concludes that they should not slander the lover of gain. Socrates compares that person to the pentathlete, who loses to the specialist in each event. They arrive at a knowledge of how to make human beings better, the knowledge a good ruler would have, and they agree that this, rather than dabbling, is what philosophy comes to. The companion says law is the resolution of a city. Socrates answers that some resolutions are good and some are wicked, while law is meant to be something worthy, and that different cities and the same city at different times resolve opposite things, although what is real does not change in that way. He compares law to the finding of a doctor or a farmer who knows the subject. Law, he says, wants to be the discovery of what is. The companion is startled to hear Minos, the Cretan king, praised, because tragedy presents him as savage. Socrates says Minos conversed every nine years with Zeus, and that he gave Crete the laws which Lycurgus later studied for Sparta. Homer and Hesiod praise him. The Athenian tragedians slandered him because Athens had gone to war with Crete. The companion asks what makes a good lawgiver. Socrates says the one who can sow virtue in souls. The talk breaks off as he returns to who that person is.Love: Socrates asks a companion what law is and argues that true law aims at the truth. They are an Athenian stranger, Clinias of Crete, and Megillus of Sparta. The Athenian asks whether a god or a man is given the credit for their laws, and the talk opens into a day-long argument about what law is for. Clinias says the laws of Crete aim at victory in war, because every city is always at war with every other. The Athenian answers that the best law looks to the whole of virtue, not to courage alone, and that peace, not war, is the serious thing. He even defends supervised drinking as a way of testing character, and he uses the histories of Persia and of Athens to show how freedom and authority have to be held together. Clinias then admits he has been appointed, with nine others, to frame laws for a new colony, and he asks the Athenian to build the city in speech with him. They fix the households, the land, marriage, schooling, and the officials. Every law is to open with a prelude, so that a citizen obeys because he understands and not only because he is afraid. Later the Athenian argues, against those who say the world is only matter, that soul, which moves itself, is older than body, and that the gods care for human affairs. He sets out the punishments for impiety, theft, and murder. The work ends with a nocturnal council of the oldest and the best, trained in mathematics and in the single aim of the laws, and with the question of how that council is to be given the power to keep the city pointed at virtue.Love: Three old men, from Athens, Crete and Sparta, walk to a shrine of Zeus and draft laws for a new city. Farming, building, hunting, imitation, medicine, navigation, and generalship are all useful, and none of them is wisdom. He turns to number. A creature that could not count could not be brave, temperate, or just, and would have no account to give of anything. Number was given to human beings so that they could think. They are not a single conversation, and they are not all of one quality. The Seventh is the long one. It answers the friends of Dion, who has been murdered, and it tells Plato's own story: his disillusion with the Thirty Tyrants in Athens, his judgment that the cities of his time were all badly governed, and his repeated visits to Syracuse, first under Dionysius I and then, at Dion's urging, under Dionysius II. He describes the philosophical test he set the younger Dionysius, the ruler's shallow enthusiasm, the quarrels, Plato's detention in the palace, and his eventual escape. In the middle of the narrative he pauses to say what he has never written. The things he really cares about cannot be put in a book. Understanding is kindled in the soul by long companionship, suddenly, like a light struck from a fire. He also warns Dion's party against taking revenge in a way that would repeat the disease they had meant to cure.
