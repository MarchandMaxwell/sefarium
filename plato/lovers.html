Lovers Ἐρασταί

Plato · Tetralogy IV · 132a–139a

Two young men who love the same boy argue before Socrates about what it means to love wisdom.

About This Text

Dramatic date No dramatic date. A schoolroom, with no historical marker. Traditionally doubted.

Socrates goes into the school of Dionysius the grammarian and finds two boys arguing, drawing circles and imitating the angles with their hands, apparently over Anaxagoras or Oenopides. He asks the young man beside him, a lover of one of the boys, what they are so serious about. The lover says it is idle chatter about things in the sky. Another young man, a rival for the same boy and a student of music, interrupts and says philosophy is a fine thing. Socrates asks them what philosophy is. The athletic lover treats it as a smattering of many arts, enough to follow a conversation and not enough to be a craftsman. Socrates compares that person to the pentathlete, who loses to the specialist in each event. He asks what knowledge would let someone assign each specialist his work and judge whether it is well done. They arrive at a knowledge of how to make human beings better, the knowledge a good ruler would have, and they agree that this, rather than dabbling, is what philosophy comes to. Socrates leaves the point there.

Love in this dialogue

In a school, two rivals compete for a boy's attention, one an athlete and one well read. Socrates asks whether loving wisdom means knowing a lot. It means knowing how to make oneself and others better.
